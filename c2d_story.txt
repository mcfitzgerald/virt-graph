C2D Virtual Twin Demo — User Story

  The Setup

  A CSCO sits down at a screen. He sees his supply chain — not a PowerPoint, not a dashboard of disconnected KPIs — but his actual business as
  a connected system. Everything he's looking at was generated from his existing ERP. Nothing was migrated, rebuilt, or modeled by consultants.

  Act 1: "Here's Your Business" (Visibility × Scope)

  He sees a network map of his supply chain. 38 entity types, 50 relationships, organized into four domains: Procurement, Supply, Demand, and a
   cross-cutting Finance/Orchestration layer. He can see at a glance that 16 relationships cross domain boundaries — these are the seams where
  visibility typically breaks down.

  He clicks on "Demand" and the map filters to show orders, shipments, invoices, returns — all connected. He sees three channels feeding
  through different logistics paths to different store types. He already knows this, but he's never seen it as a single connected picture
  derived directly from his systems.

  What this illustrates: The ontology is the twin. It's a machine-readable map of the business that makes all the connections explicit. It
  doesn't replace the ERP — it makes the ERP queryable as a network.

  Act 2: "Here's What's Happening" (Visibility × Fidelity)

  He clicks into a cost-to-deliver waterfall. Same premium toothpaste, three channels:

  - Club: 54% margin
  - Grocery: 38% margin
  - Convenience: 22% margin

  He already suspected this. But now he drills in. The margin gap isn't price — Convenience actually charges more per case than Grocery. The
  destruction happens in six places:

  1. Freight: $1.60/case to a Club warehouse. $15.00/case last-mile to a Manhattan bodega. Same truck, same product, 9x cost.
  2. Trade spend: Different discount structures by channel. Club gets volume pricing, Convenience gets shelf-space premiums.
  3. Returns: Convenience returns at 5x the rate of Club. Most come back damaged or expired.
  4. Payment terms: Club pays in 15 days. Convenience pays in 70. That's working capital drag that never appears in a P&L.
  5. Yield variance: The Citrus Premix line is running 83% yield when it should be 97%. That's 15% material waste buried in COGS.
  6. Supplier price creep: The HDPE bottle supplier is invoicing $0.72 when the PO says $0.68. $1,480 in open variances that nobody has
  escalated.

  He clicks on the freight bar and the network map highlights the three-leg shipment path from plant to store. He clicks on a specific
  Convenience store and sees that within Convenience, Manhattan costs $15/case while Philadelphia costs $7.20. A 2x spread inside the same
  channel.

  What this illustrates: The twin connects data across domains that normally live in separate systems. Freight is in TMS, invoice variances are
   in AP, yield is in MES, DSO is in AR. The ontology is the shared language that lets you query across all of them in a single waterfall.

  Act 3: "Here's How It Flows" (Visibility × Time)

  He picks the worst-margin Convenience order and watches it trace end-to-end through the business:

  Procurement → A purchase order goes to the HDPE supplier at $0.68/unit. The goods receipt confirms delivery. The AP invoice comes in at
  $0.72. A variance is flagged.

  Manufacturing → A work order triggers a Citrus Premix batch. Yield comes in at 83%. More material consumed, less output produced. The system
  knows this violates the expected 96-98% range.

  Logistics → Three shipment legs: plant to regional DC ($0.80/case), regional DC to customer DC ($2.50/case), customer DC to a Manhattan
  bodega ($15.00/case). Each leg has a state: planned → in-transit → delivered.

  Finance → The AR invoice goes out. Payment terms are Net-60. The customer pays on day 70. The GL journal records balanced debits and credits
  at every stage.

  At each handoff, the system checks conservation: cases ordered = cases shipped = cases invoiced. If they don't match, it flags the
  discrepancy. The rules for what should be true are declared in the twin, not buried in a report someone runs quarterly.

  What this illustrates: A twin isn't a snapshot — it's a system that understands how things flow. Material flows forward, money flows
  backward, and at every boundary the system can verify that the rules of the business are being respected. The state machines and flow
  declarations are part of the ontology, not hardcoded logic.

  Act 4: "Here's What You Can Do About It" (Simulation)

  Now the CSCO starts asking questions:

  "What if we fix the yield problem?" He adjusts Citrus Premix yield from 83% to 97%. The waterfall recalculates. COGS drops. He sees exactly
  which SKUs are affected and by how much.

  "What if we renegotiate the HDPE price back to contract?" He adjusts from $0.72 to $0.68. The system traces through: AP invoice amounts
  change, invoice variances clear, COGS adjusts, margins improve by a specific dollar amount.

  "What if we drop the Manhattan Convenience stores?" He removes two nodes from the network. The system recalculates: which orders are
  affected, how freight allocation changes for remaining stores, what happens to Convenience margins overall. The map updates to show the
  simplified network. Centrality scores shift — maybe a DC that was critical before is now underutilized.

  "What if we consolidate from 3 DCs to 2?" The system traces the impact through the transport graph. Some store-level freight goes up, some
  goes down. The net effect flows through to the waterfall. Conservation checks verify that all orders can still be fulfilled through the
  remaining network.

  Each scenario is validated against the declared rules: shipment dates must be after production dates, cases must be conserved across
  handoffs, GL must balance. A slider can't produce an impossible state — the twin knows the constraints.

  What this illustrates: The twin doesn't just show you data — it knows the structure of your business well enough to reason about changes. The
   ontology declares which parameters are tunable, what the valid ranges are, and which downstream entities are impacted. Scenario analysis
  isn't a separate model — it's a natural consequence of having a complete map.

  Act 5: "And None of This Required a Migration" (The Close)

  Everything the CSCO just saw runs on his existing ERP tables. The ontology is a YAML file — version-controlled, auditable, evolvable. When
  the business changes (new channel, new supplier, new product line), the map updates. When a new question comes up that nobody anticipated,
  the connected structure lets you traverse to the answer instead of building a new report.

  The six cost drivers the CSCO just explored live in six different systems today. The twin makes them one system — not by moving data, but by
  making the connections explicit and queryable.

⏺ Here's how it works:

  The Architecture

  OntologyAccessor at src/virt_graph/ontology.py:43 does two things at construction time:

  1. Loads the metamodel rules (lines 132-184) — reads virt_graph.yaml (the VG extension vocabulary) via LinkML's SchemaView to discover what
  annotations are required and what enum values are valid. This is cached once per process. The metamodel is the single source of truth for
  validation rules — the code doesn't hardcode what's required, it reads it from the YAML.

  2. Parses the domain ontology (lines 98-130):
  - yaml.safe_load() — loads the raw YAML for direct annotation access
  - SchemaView(path, merge_imports=True) — loads through LinkML's resolver, which follows imports: scm_base and merges inherited slots from
  abstract classes and mixins
  - _index_classes() (line 200) — partitions everything into TBox (entities that instantiate vg:SQLMappedClass) and RBox (relationships that
  instantiate vg:SQLMappedRelationship)

  The Key Insight

  The partition is driven by a single field in the YAML — instantiates:

  # In pcg.yaml — this is an entity (TBox)
  Supplier:
    instantiates: [vg:SQLMappedClass]
    annotations:
      vg:table: suppliers
      vg:primary_key: id
      vg:context: {...}
      vg:state_machine: {...}

  # This is a relationship (RBox)
  SupplierOffersIngredient:
    instantiates: [vg:SQLMappedRelationship]
    annotations:
      vg:edge_table: supplier_ingredients
      vg:domain_key: supplier_id
      vg:range_key: ingredient_id
      vg:operation_types: [direct_join]
      vg:flow_config: {...}

  Everything downstream — get_class_table(), get_operation_types(), get_role_flow_config(), get_class_state_machine() — is just reading
  annotations from the indexed TBox/RBox dictionaries. The _get_annotation() helper (line 233) handles the vg: prefix stripping and the
  _parse_json_or_value() helper (line 250) handles annotations that are JSON strings vs raw values.

  What This Means for the UI

  The ontology accessor is already a complete read API. A React frontend with a thin Python API layer can call:

  - get_all_domains() → renders the 4-domain network map
  - get_class_state_machine("Order") → renders state machine diagrams
  - get_role_flow_config("BatchConsumesIngredient") → renders flow arrows with types
  - get_conservation_groups() → groups entities into traceable business processes
  - get_class_scenario_params("Plant") → generates slider controls for what-if

  The ontology is the API contract for the UI. Every panel, every interaction, every drill-down is backed by a method that already exists on
  this class.

    Why an Ontology Makes This Possible

  The C2D waterfall is a business question, not a database query. No single table answers it. The answer lives in the relationships between 15+
   tables across 3 domains. Without an ontology, you need a human who holds the mental model of the schema in their head and hand-writes a
  200-line SQL join. With an ontology, the reasoning system can discover the answer path itself.

  The Problem: Latent Structure

  The PCG database has 41 tables. A raw schema dump gives you column names and types — shipments.freight_cost, ar_invoice_lines.line_amount,
  skus.cost_per_case. But nothing in the DDL tells you:

  - That ar_invoice_lines.line_amount is net revenue (post-deduction), not gross
  - That order_lines.unit_price is always zero and is a trap
  - That freight has 3 legs but only the last one links to an AR invoice
  - That the other 2 legs must be allocated proportionally to channels
  - That shipments.origin_id is polymorphic — it could point to a plant, a DC, or a retail location, and the resolution rule lives in
  origin_type
  - That trade deductions decompose the gap between list price and invoiced price, and the sum must reconcile exactly

  All of this is latent knowledge — it exists in the heads of the people who built the system. The ontology makes it explicit and
  machine-readable.

  What the Ontology Actually Provides

  1. The navigation graph — "what connects to what and how"

  The RBox (57 relationships) is a map. Each relationship declares its edge table, domain/range keys, and domain/range classes. An agentic
  system reading ARInvoiceForShipment knows: start at ar_invoices, join on shipment_id to reach shipments. It doesn't need to guess which _id
  column goes where — the ontology says so explicitly.

  This is the difference between a schema (which says shipment_id INTEGER) and an ontology (which says ARInvoice → Shipment via
  ar_invoices.shipment_id, functional, direct_join).

  2. The semantic warnings — "what NOT to do"

  The vg:context blocks carry hard-won domain knowledge. The SKU context says:

  "WARNING: order_lines.unit_price is ALWAYS ZERO — use ar_invoice_lines for actual revenue."

  The ARInvoice context says:

  "total_amount reflects net revenue post-deductions"

  The freight allocation methodology says:

  "Only customer_dc_to_store shipments have AR invoice links; upstream legs must be allocated proportionally"

  Without these, the reasoning system would naively join order_lines.unit_price, get zeros, and produce a wrong answer confidently. The
  ontology encodes the traps and gotchas that a new analyst would learn only after weeks of painful debugging.

  3. The analytical methodology — "how to compose the answer"

  The vg:analytical_methodology annotation is a recipe at the schema level. It doesn't just name the tables — it declares the formula, the
  correct source columns, and the invariants that must hold. When the system reads:

  "invariant": "For every AR invoice: SUM(trade_deductions.deduction_amount)
                = gross_revenue - net_revenue exactly"

  ...it now has a self-check. It can compute the waterfall AND verify it reconciles. This is the ontology telling the system not just what to
  compute but how to know if the answer is right.

  4. Polymorphic resolution — "the join that isn't a join"

  The 3-leg freight problem is the hardest part of the waterfall. ShipmentFromOrigin and ShipmentToDestination are polymorphic relationships —
  origin_id can point to a Plant, DC, or RetailLocation. The ontology declares:

  vg:range_class: '["Plant", "DistributionCenter", "RetailLocation"]'

  with context explaining that origin_type values are supplier/plant/rdc/customer_dc/store. Without this, the system would try to join
  origin_id to a single table and get garbage. The ontology tells it to branch on origin_type and resolve to 3 different tables — and the
  context tells it the actual discriminator values in the data (which differ from what you'd naively guess).

  5. Cross-domain bridges — "how the waterfall stitches together"

  The C2D waterfall crosses all 3 business domains:
  - Supply (product master): SKU.price_per_case, SKU.cost_per_case
  - Demand (customer-facing): AR invoices, shipments, returns, trade deductions
  - Procurement (upstream): inbound freight legs originating from plants

  The ontology flags 18 relationships as vg:cross_domain: true. These are the bridge joints — ARInvoiceLineForSKU connects demand-side revenue
  to supply-side product, ShipmentFromOrigin connects demand-side logistics to supply-side plants. The vg:inter_domain_flows annotation maps
  the material and financial flows that cross these boundaries.

  A system reading the ontology can discover that a C2D waterfall requires traversing domain boundaries at specific relationship crossings — it
   doesn't need to be told the full join path in advance.

  6. Operation type dispatch — "SQL or handler?"

  Most of the waterfall is direct_join — standard SQL. But the ontology also declares that some relationships require handlers
  (recursive_traversal for SKU alias chains, shortest_path for transport network). The system reads vg:operation_types and knows whether to
  generate SQL or call a Python handler. For the C2D waterfall specifically, everything is direct_join, so it's pure SQL — but the system knows
   this because the ontology told it so, not because it guessed.

  The Punchline

  Without the ontology, you need a domain expert to write:

  -- 200 lines of carefully ordered JOINs, CASE statements for polymorphic
  -- resolution, proportional freight allocation CTEs, trade deduction
  -- aggregation, return cost netting... written by someone who knows that
  -- order_lines.unit_price is zero, that freight has 3 legs, that origin_id
  -- is polymorphic, and that ar_invoice_lines.line_amount is already net.

  With the ontology, a general-purpose reasoning system can:
  1. Read the analytical_methodology — learn the formula and invariants
  2. Read the TBox — discover which tables hold each waterfall component
  3. Read the RBox — find the join paths between them
  4. Read the vg:context blocks — avoid the traps
  5. Resolve polymorphism — handle the multi-leg freight correctly
  6. Self-verify — check that trade deductions reconcile, GL balances, totals match

  The ontology is the transfer medium for domain expertise. It takes what lives in one person's head and makes it available to any reasoning
  system that can read YAML. That's the raison d'etre: not just "what tables exist" but "how this business actually works, and how to not get
  it wrong."
